1. History and Overview

A stock exchange is a queue with rules, and every change in its four-hundred-year history has been a change to the rules of the queue rather than to the idea of one. Brokers who once shouted bids across a room now send binary structs across a switch. The function did not change. The queue got faster, and the rules that decide who is served first got written down.

Everything in this document follows from that. Who stands where in the queue is the entire product.

1.1 The Club, 1792 to 1971

The New York Stock Exchange began as a cartel and spent its first two centuries defending that structure. The Buttonwood Agreement, signed by 24 stockbrokers on 17 May 1792, did two things: it committed the signatories to trade with each other in preference to outsiders, and it fixed commissions. A constitution adopted on 8 March 1817 created the New York Stock and Exchange Board, which rented a room at 40 Wall Street and called a list of 30 securities from a podium twice a day while brokers bid from assigned chairs. That is where the word "seat" comes from.

Two inventions turned a room into a market. The stock ticker, introduced in 1867, made the last sale visible outside the building. Telephones, installed in 1878, let orders arrive from anywhere. On 15 December 1886 volume passed one million shares for the first time.

Congress created the Securities and Exchange Commission in 1934 to supervise what the exchanges had been doing to themselves. The structure survived intact until the 1970s: one venue, one auction, fixed commissions, human intermediation.

1.2 The Break, 1971 to 2001

Nasdaq broke the model by not being an exchange. When it opened in 1971 it was a quotation display system: competing dealers published bids and offers, and a trade happened when a broker telephoned a dealer and agreed. There was no central book and no matching engine. The screen showed prices; the humans made the trades.

Congress ordered the pieces joined. The 1975 amendments to the Securities Exchange Act added Section 11A, directing the SEC to facilitate a national market system, and the Consolidated Tape Association has disseminated a single combined tape for NYSE-listed and regional-listed stocks since the late 1970s. One tape, many venues. That architecture is still in place today, and Section 11 of this document explains why it is the most contested piece of American market structure.

Three changes in five years then dismantled dealer economics. The SEC's order handling rules, which took effect in 1997, forced dealers to display customer limit orders that improved their own quotes, which put electronic communication networks onto the public tape. Regulation ATS, adopted in 1998 and codified at Rules 300 through 303 of Regulation ATS, let a trading system that meets the statutory definition of an exchange operate as a registered broker-dealer instead, provided it files Form ATS and complies with the rules. Decimalisation in 2001 cut the minimum quoting increment from one sixteenth of a dollar, 6.25 cents, to one cent.

The dealer spread was the dealer's revenue. Cutting the tick by 84% removed most of it.

1.3 The Rulebook That Made the Modern Market, 2005

Regulation NMS, adopted 9 June 2005 and effective 29 August 2005, is the document that produced the market described in the rest of this research. It contains four operative rules. Rule 610 requires fair access to quotations and caps the fee a venue may charge to execute against its own protected quote. Rule 611, the Order Protection Rule, forbids a trading center from executing a trade at a price inferior to a protected quotation displayed elsewhere. Rule 612 sets the minimum pricing increment. Rules 601 and 603 govern market data consolidation and distribution.

Compliance was phased, and the schedule slipped by a year. The adopting release set a Phase I of 29 June 2006, covering 100 NMS stocks from Networks A and C and 50 from Network B, and a Phase II of 31 August 2006 covering all NMS stocks. Neither date took effect. The Commission extended them twice, at 71 FR 30038 on 24 May 2006 and again at 72 FR 4202 on 30 January 2007, the second time at the NYSE's request because its Hybrid Market rollout was late. The schedule that actually ran was a Specifications Date of 16 October 2006, a Trading Phase Date of 5 March 2007, a Pilot Stocks Phase Date of 9 July 2007, an All Stocks Phase Date of 20 August 2007, and a Completion Date of 8 October 2007. The modern protected-quote regime is a 2007 artifact, not a 2006 one.

Rule 611 did something subtle and enormous. By requiring every venue to respect the best displayed price on every other venue, it made a new exchange viable the day it opened, because its quote was legally protected from the moment it was the best. Fragmentation was not an accident of Reg NMS. It was the design.

2. What an Exchange Actually Is (and Is Not)

2.1 The Precise Definition

An exchange is four things bolted together, and only the first is the part people picture.

A matching engine. A deterministic single-threaded process that consumes a sequenced stream of order events, applies a published priority rule, and emits a sequenced stream of executions and book updates. Determinism is the product: given the same inputs in the same order, it must produce the same outputs, because that is the only way a disputed fill can be adjudicated.

A rulebook. A filed, SEC-approved document that defines every order type, every priority rule, every fee, and every halt condition. In the United States a national securities exchange is a self-regulatory organisation, and it cannot change how the engine behaves without filing a proposed rule change under Section 19(b) of the Exchange Act and Rule 19b-4.

A market data publisher. Every accepted order, cancel, and execution is broadcast. The exchange's proprietary feed carries full depth of book; the exchange must also feed the same information to the consolidated processors, and it may not send its own customers the data any sooner than it sends the processors.

A membership and clearing perimeter. Only registered broker-dealers with an established clearing relationship may connect. The exchange never takes principal risk in an equity trade and never holds customer money. Settlement is somebody else's problem.

2.2 Misconception One: An Exchange Is Not the Price

The most common error is treating an exchange's quote as the market price. It is not. It is one venue's best bid and offer, and it is legally meaningless on its own.

The number that matters is the National Best Bid and Offer, the NBBO, which is the highest protected bid and the lowest protected offer across all trading centers. The NBBO is computed by the two securities information processors, not by any exchange. The CTA/CQ processor, operated by the Securities Industry Automation Corporation, covers Tape A (NYSE-listed), administered by the NYSE, and Tape B (NYSE Arca, NYSE American, Cboe and other regional listings), administered by NYSE American. The UTP processor, operated by Nasdaq, covers Tape C (Nasdaq-listed).

A single exchange's book can be crossed against another's, and frequently is for microseconds. Only the consolidated view is the market. This distinction is the whole subject of Section 11.

2.3 Misconception Two: Not Every Exchange Is Price-Time Priority

Textbooks teach price-time priority as if it were a law of nature. It is a design choice, and the largest listing venue in the world does not use it.

Nasdaq ranks resting orders in price-time priority, exactly as taught. The NYSE does not. On the NYSE, an order that sets a new NBBO earns Setter Priority and trades its full displayed quantity ahead of everyone else at that price. Once Setter Priority interest is exhausted, remaining shares are allocated on parity: an allocation wheel at each price rotates round lots among the Designated Market Maker, each Floor Broker's electronic quote, and the aggregate electronic book, which is itself ranked in time priority as a single participant. The exchange maintains one wheel for displayed liquidity and one for non-displayed liquidity at every price.

The practical consequence is large. On Nasdaq, being 100 microseconds earlier at a price can be the difference between a fill and nothing. On the NYSE, a Floor Broker who arrives late still gets a round lot on the next rotation, because parity divides the incoming order across categories of participant rather than ordering it strictly by arrival time.

Two exchanges, two answers to the same question. Neither is wrong; they are optimising for different liquidity providers.

2.4 What an Exchange Is Not

Not a counterparty. An equity exchange matches and reports. The National Securities Clearing Corporation novates the trade and becomes the counterparty to both sides. If a member fails, the exchange loses nothing; the clearing house does.

Not a custodian. Shares live at the Depository Trust Company in book entry. The exchange never touches them.

Not the venue of last resort. Half of US share volume prints off-exchange through the FINRA Trade Reporting Facilities, which are trade reporting mechanisms rather than trading venues. The exchange sets the reference price for trades it does not execute.

Not a fee-neutral utility. Exchange revenue comes from transaction fees, market data, and connectivity, in roughly that order of contentiousness. The pricing of the second and third items has been the subject of continuous litigation for fifteen years.

Not able to change its own behaviour. A US exchange cannot add an order type, change an allocation rule, or alter a fee without an SEC filing that is published for comment. This is why exchange technology moves slowly relative to its engineering capability, and why order type documentation is unusually reliable.

2.5 The Simplest Accurate Mental Model

An exchange is a sorted multimap from price to a FIFO queue of orders, plus a publish loop, plus a rulebook that says how ties break. Everything else in this research is an elaboration of that sentence, a regulatory constraint on it, or an economic consequence of it.

3.2 The Two Roles That Determine Where Volume Goes

The smart order router decides everything. No investor chooses a venue. A router does, in software, on a per-child-order basis, weighing the displayed size at each venue, the fee or rebate, the probability of a fill, the risk of information leakage, and the latency to each matching engine. Exchanges do not compete for orders; they compete for a line of code inside twenty routers.

The wholesaler decides whether retail volume reaches an exchange at all. A retail market order in the United States usually never touches an exchange. It is routed to a wholesaler that executes it as principal, at a price at or inside the NBBO, and reports the print to a FINRA TRF. The exchange supplies the reference price and receives none of the trade. This is the mechanism behind the 50.16% off-exchange figure, and Section 14 takes it apart.

3.3 The Role That Exists Only at One Exchange

The Designated Market Maker is a role no electronic exchange has, and its persistence at the NYSE is the single largest structural difference in US equity market structure. There is exactly one DMM per NYSE-listed security. Three firms hold all of them: Citadel Securities, Virtu Americas, and GTS Securities.

Three firms are the human backstop for every NYSE listing. Section 10 explains what they actually do, and why the number is three rather than thirty.

4. The Limit Order Book as a Data Structure

4.1 What the Book Is

A limit order book is a sorted map from price level to a first-in-first-out queue of resting orders, held twice, once for each side. The bid side sorts descending, the ask side ascending. The best bid and best offer are the front of each map. An incoming order either crosses the opposite side and executes, or it does not and joins the back of a queue.

That is the whole abstraction. Every optimisation below exists because the abstraction has to survive a million messages a second.

4.2 Why the Naive Implementation Fails

The obvious implementation is a balanced tree keyed by price, with a linked list at each node. It works and it is too slow, for a reason that is about memory rather than asymptotics.

A red-black tree lookup is O(log n) but every step is a pointer dereference into a different cache line. At the message rates a US equity engine sustains, the dominant cost is cache misses, not comparisons. The CTA SIP's CQS quote feed peaked at 1,754,000 messages per second in July 2026 across Tapes A and B. One venue's own outbound rate is not published, but Nasdaq requires a 10 Gb or 40 Gb connection to carry the hardware version of TotalView-ITCH, which is the published evidence of the order of magnitude a single exchange emits. A design that misses cache on every price lookup does not survive that.

Production engines therefore make four substitutions.

Prices become array indices. Prices are integers in ticks, not floating point. Nasdaq's ITCH and OUCH protocols both carry prices as unsigned integers with four implied decimal places, so 190.0400 is the integer 1900400. A book allocates a contiguous array covering a plausible price range around the last sale and indexes it directly. Lookup becomes one multiply and one add. If the price moves outside the window, the array is re-centred, which is rare and can be amortised.

Order queues become intrusive doubly linked lists. The order object itself carries the prev and next pointers rather than being wrapped in a list node. Cancelling then requires no search: given the node, unlink in constant time.

Order identity becomes a dense index. Every exchange assigns its own day-unique order reference number. ITCH's Add Order message carries an 8-byte Order Reference Number at offset 11; OUCH's Order Accepted message carries the same value at offset 36. Internally the engine keeps a slot array or an open-addressed hash keyed by that number, so a cancel or replace resolves to a node in one probe.

Aggregates are maintained, never computed. Total displayed size at a level, order count at a level, and the current best bid and offer are all counters updated on every book mutation. Nothing scans.

The result is that add, cancel, and execute are all effectively constant time with one or two cache misses each.

4.3 Displayed, Non-Displayed, and Reserve

A price level is not one queue. It is at least three, and their relative priority is a rulebook decision.

Displayed orders are published on the market data feed and count toward the exchange's protected quote. Only displayed orders receive Rule 611 protection.

Non-displayed orders rest in the book and execute but never appear on the feed. They are invisible to everyone including the router that just missed them.

Reserve orders, the exchange's name for an iceberg, have a displayed portion and a hidden remainder. Nasdaq's rule is explicit: a reserve order carries a round lot display size and a corresponding non-display size; incoming order flow reaches both; the minimum displayed quantity is 100 shares and it is replenished when the displayed amount falls below 100. On OUCH, the displayed portion is set with the MaxFloor option, tag 5, a 4-byte integer, and RandomReserves, tag 13, randomises the replenishment size so the refresh pattern cannot be fingerprinted.

At a given price, US exchanges generally rank displayed ahead of non-displayed. Nasdaq's Supplemental Order, a non-displayed order that always executes at the NBBO, is explicitly the lowest priority at its price level, behind both displayed and other non-displayed interest.

The refresh is the tell. When an iceberg's displayed 100 shares are taken and 100 more appear at the same price with a new timestamp, a participant watching the ITCH feed sees an Order Executed message followed by an Add Order message at the same price a few microseconds later. Detecting icebergs from the message stream is a standard exercise, which is why RandomReserves exists.

4.4 The Book Is Reconstructed, Not Received

The exchange does not send the book. It sends the events, and the subscriber builds the book.

Nasdaq TotalView-ITCH 5.0 is an order-level feed. It sends A (Add Order, 36 bytes) or F (Add Order with MPID attribution, 40 bytes) when an order joins the displayed book. It then sends E (Order Executed), C (Order Executed With Price), X (Order Cancel, a partial reduction), D (Order Delete, full removal), or U (Order Replace) as that order changes. The specification is blunt about the arithmetic: modify messages state the number of shares affected, and the subscriber must deduct that from the original Add Order quantity; multiple modify messages for the same order reference number are cumulative; when displayed shares reach zero, the order is dead and must be removed.

Order Replace assigns a new order reference number, and the specification warns that the side, symbol, and MPID are not repeated in the message. The subscriber must retain them from the original Add Order. A book builder that forgets this drops the side of every replaced order.

There is also a category of execution that never appears as an Add. Non-displayed orders produce no Add Order message; when they trade, the feed carries a P Trade message instead, which reports the print but does not affect the book. Auction executions arrive as a single bulk Q Cross Trade message per symbol. A firm building a book must process A/F/E/C/X/D/U and ignore P and Q; a firm building a time-and-sales tape must do the opposite and must also honour the Printable flag on C messages, because non-printable executions are rolled into a later bulk print and counting both double-counts volume.

4.5 The Book at One Level of Detail Lower

Real engines shard by symbol, not by function. A matching engine process owns a set of symbols and runs one thread per shard, because a single symbol's book must be mutated serially to stay deterministic. Cross-symbol atomicity is not required for equities, which is what makes the sharding trivially correct.

Everything upstream of the shard is I/O and everything downstream is publication. The matching itself is a single-threaded loop over a queue, and it has been for twenty years, because the alternative loses determinism and determinism is the regulated property.

5. Order Types and Time in Force

5.1 The Two Primitives

Every order type in every rulebook is a composition of two decisions: at what price will this trade, and how long does the instruction live.

A market order names no price. It executes against whatever is resting, walking the book until filled. It carries unbounded price risk, which is exactly the property that made it dangerous on 6 May 2010.

A limit order names a worst acceptable price. It executes at that price or better, and if it cannot execute it rests. A limit order to buy at 190.03 may execute at 190.02; it may never execute at 190.04.

Everything else is one of these two with conditions attached.

5.2 Stop Orders Are Not Book Orders

A stop order is the type most often misunderstood, and the misunderstanding matters. A stop order is not resting liquidity. It is a conditional instruction held outside the book, invisible to everyone, that converts into a live market order or limit order when a trigger price prints.

Two consequences follow. A stop order provides no depth: a book that looks thin might have a million shares of stops behind it that nobody can see and nobody can trade against. And a stop market order, once triggered, has no price floor at all, so it will execute against whatever remains, however far away.

On 6 May 2010 both properties fired at once. Retail stop-loss orders triggered as prices fell, converted into market orders, found no bids, and executed against stub quotes at a penny. The SEC and CFTC report is explicit that sell orders in individual securities, "including many retail stop-loss orders, triggered by declines in prices of those securities," found reduced buying interest and drove prices lower still.

5.3 Pegged Orders

A pegged order does not carry a price. It carries a rule for deriving one, and the engine reprices it as the reference moves. Nasdaq supports four peg types, encoded in the OUCH PriceType option, tag 6, one byte: P market peg, M midpoint peg, R primary peg, Q market maker peg, with L limit as the default. A signed PegOffset, tag 7, four bytes, shifts the derived price by a fixed amount in one-cent increments.

Primary peg tracks the same-side NBBO. A buy pegged primary sits at the national best bid and follows it up and down. It provides displayed depth without requiring the sender to chase.

Market peg tracks the opposite-side NBBO. A buy pegged to the market sits at the national best offer, which means it is willing to pay the spread continuously.

Midpoint peg sits at the NBBO midpoint. This is the workhorse of institutional trading, because a midpoint execution splits the spread: both sides do better than the displayed quote. When the spread is an odd number of pennies the midpoint falls on a half cent, and exchanges are permitted to price these orders in half-penny increments even while the minimum quoting increment stays a penny. That asymmetry, executing in half pennies while quoting in whole pennies, is one of the reasons the SEC moved on tick sizes in 2024.

Pegged orders interact with the volatility bands. Nasdaq's rule is that a pegged order whose instruction would price it more aggressively than the LULD band is repriced to the band; if the peg lands it passively outside the band, it stays but is not executable until the band moves.

5.4 The IEX Speed Bump and the Crumbling Quote

Midpoint pegs have a specific vulnerability: the reference price they follow can be stale. If the NBBO is in the middle of moving down and a midpoint peg has not yet repriced, a faster participant can trade against the old midpoint and take a risk-free fraction of a cent.

IEX built an exchange around solving exactly that. Every inbound order and cancellation traverses 44 miles of coiled optical fiber, plus the network path between IEX's point of presence and its matching engine, which together impose a 350-microsecond delay. IEX's own market data intake is not delayed. The exchange therefore always sees the rest of the market before it acts on any order it has received. The coil was originally 38 miles; after IEX relocated its primary data centre in 2024 the fiber was lengthened to hold the delay constant at 350 microseconds.

On top of the delay sits the Crumbling Quote Indicator, which IEX calls the Signal: a formula that judges whether the National Best Bid is about to fall or the National Best Offer is about to rise. When it fires it stays on for 2 milliseconds or until the price changes. Two order types consume it, Discretionary Peg and Discretionary Limit, which decline to trade aggressively while the quote is judged unstable.

IEX traded 4.43% of US share volume in July 2026, more than any Cboe venue.

5.5 Post-Only, Price Sliding, and ISO

Three order behaviours exist purely to satisfy Regulation NMS, and they are invisible in any textbook description of a limit order book.

Post-only orders refuse to remove liquidity. On Nasdaq a post-only order that would lock the book posts one tick away from the opposite best price; one that would cross the book executes at the resting order's price, which means the incoming order receives price improvement rather than paying the spread. The purpose is economic: adding liquidity earns a rebate, removing it pays a fee, and a router that wants the rebate cannot risk accidentally taking.

Price sliding handles the locked-and-crossed prohibition. Nasdaq's Price-To-Comply order reprices to comply with Rule 611 and the locked-market rule: a buy is priced at the inside offer but displayed one tick below it. When the previously impermissible price becomes available, the member chooses among three behaviours by port configuration: leave the order at the old price as non-displayed while keeping its timestamp, cancel it back so the firm can re-enter it as displayed with a new timestamp, or display it automatically at the new price with a new timestamp. Every one of those choices trades priority against visibility.

Intermarket Sweep Orders are the compliance escape hatch. Rule 600(b)(47) defines an ISO as a limit order that is marked as an ISO when routed, and that is sent simultaneously with one or more additional limit orders, also marked ISO, routed to execute against all better-priced protected quotations at other trading centers up to their displayed size. Rule 611(b)(5) then lets the receiving venue execute the ISO immediately without regard to protected quotes elsewhere, because the sender has asserted it already swept them. On OUCH the assertion is one byte at offset 29: Y eligible, N not eligible.

The ISO is the mechanism that lets a large order execute in microseconds across a fragmented market. It is also an assertion the receiving exchange cannot verify; Rule 611(c) places the obligation on the router to take reasonable steps to establish that the order actually qualifies.

6.2 Price-Time, and What It Rewards

Under strict price-time priority the queue at each price is a FIFO. The order that arrived first is filled completely before the second receives a share. Nasdaq's OUCH specification states it plainly: non-matching orders "may be added to the NASDAQ Limit Order Book, a database of available limit orders, where they wait to be matched in price-time priority."

Price-time rewards speed absolutely. Being first in the queue at a price is worth a measurable amount, because the front of the queue is where adverse selection is lowest: an order at the front trades against the next arrival, which is on average uninformed, while an order at the back only trades after the front has been consumed, which usually means the price is about to move through the level.

Price-time also creates the cancel-replace penalty. Nasdaq's OUCH Replace Order message documents it explicitly: replacing an order always gives it a new timestamp for its time priority. A participant who wants to reduce size while keeping queue position must send a partial Cancel Order rather than a Replace, and the specification says so in the same paragraph.

6.3 Parity, and What It Rewards

The NYSE allocates differently. Its own FAQ defines parity as "the method by which NYSE allocates marketable orders against contra side orders. Incoming marketable orders execute against multiple parity participants rather than a single order with time priority."

Three categories of participant are eligible at each price: each Floor Broker, through electronic quotes entered from the floor; the Designated Market Maker; and the electronic book, which contains all off-floor interest and is itself ranked in time priority but participates as a single entity.

Above parity sits Setter Priority. An order that sets a new NBBO trades its full displayed quantity before any parity allocation happens. An order can gain Setter Priority on arrival or when it returns unexecuted from an away market after routing. Setter Priority is not retained in the Closing Auction or in a reopening auction after a halt.

When there is no Setter Priority interest, the exchange's matching engine maintains an allocation wheel at each price, one for displayed liquidity and one for non-displayed. The wheel tracks arrival-time ranking among parity participants and is used when an incoming order is too small to be shared. Allocation proceeds in round lots: the participant in the first wheel position takes a round lot, then the next, and so on until the aggressing order is exhausted or all participants are satisfied.

Not everything is parity eligible. Floor Broker D-Orders, Limit Day, Limit Day Non-Routable, Limit On Open, Midpoint Orders, Midpoint Peg, and Last Sale Peg qualify. Market orders, MOO, MOC, LOC and closing offset orders, yielding orders, IOC orders, and any non-displayed order carrying a minimum trade size do not.

6.4 Why the Difference Exists

Parity is a subsidy to a specific kind of liquidity provider: one that is willing to commit size but is not willing or able to win a latency race. A Floor Broker representing an institutional order cannot beat a co-located server to the front of a FIFO queue and would therefore never post on a strict price-time book. Parity guarantees that broker a share of every incoming order at its price, which makes posting rational.

Price-time is a subsidy to speed. It produces tight spreads because it produces intense competition for the front of the queue, and it produces that competition by making the front of the queue extremely valuable.

The two exchanges have chosen different liquidity providers to pay. Both choices are filed, public, and SEC-approved.

6.5 Pro-Rata, and Why Equities Do Not Use It

A third allocation model exists and dominates a different asset class. Under pro-rata allocation, an incoming order is divided among all resting orders at a price in proportion to their size, with no reference to arrival time. Options exchanges and some futures markets use it, often with a small size-priority or market-maker-priority overlay.

Pro-rata rewards size instead of speed, and it has an obvious failure mode: because a larger resting order receives a proportionally larger fill, participants inflate quoted size beyond what they actually want to trade, and the displayed book overstates real liquidity. Equity markets in the United States use price-time or parity and leave pro-rata to instruments where quoted size is naturally large and the number of price levels is small.

7.2 The Sequencer Is the Architecture

The single most important component is not the matcher. It is the sequencer, the point at which a stream of concurrent arrivals becomes one total order.

Before the sequencer, everything is parallel: many gateways, many ports, many threads, arbitrary interleaving. After the sequencer, everything is a replayable log. The matching engine is a pure function of that log. Recovery is replay. The hot standby is a second process consuming the same log and producing bit-identical state.

Nasdaq's OUCH specification describes the redundancy directly: a single OUCH account can be bound to multiple physical OUCH machines that mirror each other, both able to accept orders and cancels, with outbound messages generated simultaneously by both hosts.

The sequencer also settles a legal question. Two orders that arrive at the same time are ordered by whichever the sequencer numbered first. That is not a physical fact about the world, it is a decision the exchange made and published, and it is defensible precisely because it is arbitrary and consistent.

7.3 The Protocol Layers

Nasdaq's stack is the clearest published example of the whole path, and the layering generalises.

Order entry runs on OUCH over SoupBinTCP. OUCH messages have fixed lengths per type. Enter Order is type O: 1-byte type, 4-byte UserRefNum, 1-byte side, 4-byte quantity, 8-byte symbol, 8-byte price, then time in force, display, capacity, ISO eligibility, cross type, a 14-byte ClOrdID, a 2-byte appendage length, and a variable TagValue appendage. Integers are big-endian. Prices carry four implied decimals, with a maximum of 199,999.9900 and a sentinel of 214,748.3647, hex 7FFFFFFF, meaning "market order for a cross."

The UserRefNum design deserves attention. It is a client-assigned unsigned integer that must be unique and strictly increasing on the port throughout the day, starting at 1, and the system ignores any new order request carrying a UserRefNum lower than the last one processed, treating it as a retransmission. That single rule makes the inbound path idempotent without any server-side deduplication table. The specification is explicit about why: host-bound messages are inherently non-guaranteed even over TCP, so every one of them is designed to be benignly resendable.

Market data runs on ITCH over MoldUDP64 multicast, or over SoupBinTCP for a reliable unicast copy. ITCH messages are also fixed-length per type with big-endian integers, 6-byte timestamps expressed as nanoseconds since midnight, and prices with four implied decimals capped at 200,000.0000. Every message carries a 2-byte Stock Locate code at the same offset in every message type, a small integer assigned fresh each day starting at 1, intended as an array index for instrument lookup and positioned identically so that a subscriber can filter by symbol without decoding the rest of the message.

That is a design decision made for the benefit of an FPGA. Nasdaq broadcasts a hardware version of TotalView-ITCH from Carteret, New Jersey, over MoldUDP64 only, requiring a 10 Gb or 40 Gb connection, with an identical payload and a guaranteed identical message order to the software feed.

7.4 Where the Latency Actually Goes

The competitive number an exchange quotes is wire-to-wire latency: hardware timestamp at ingress to hardware timestamp at egress. Every exchange defines the measurement points slightly differently, which is why cross-exchange comparisons are unreliable and why this document does not present a table of them.

Absolute figures are nonetheless on the public record, and they fix the scale. In Release 34-78102, the Commission Interpretation Regarding Automated Quotations Under Regulation NMS published at 81 FR 40785 on 23 June 2016, the SEC records Nasdaq's own representation that the throughput time of Nasdaq's system is 40 microseconds, and a commenter's representation that the Bombay Stock Exchange processes a transaction in 6 microseconds. Neither is a wire-to-wire figure on a comparable basis, because no US equity exchange publishes one. The same release fixes the other end of the range: IEX's speed bump costs 350 microseconds inbound and 350 microseconds outbound, a cumulative 700 microseconds. The engine path is tens of microseconds. The deliberate delay wrapped around it is hundreds.

Those numbers also rank the components. An engine path of tens of microseconds cannot be dominated by a match that costs a handful of cache misses, which is why the list below puts the risk gate first and the match third.

The cross-connect is physics. Light travels about 20 centimetres per nanosecond in fiber. A 100-metre cross-connect inside a co-location facility costs about 500 nanoseconds each way. This is why co-location cages are priced by proximity and why the entire industry moved into four buildings in New Jersey.

The risk gate is the largest software cost. SEC Rule 15c3-5 requires pre-trade risk controls on every order reaching an exchange: credit and capital thresholds, price collars, duplicate detection, and message rate limits. These are per-account state lookups on the critical path. Most of the engineering effort in a modern gateway goes into making them cache-resident.

The match itself is a handful of cache misses. Given the data structures in Section 4, matching an order against the front of a queue is a small number of memory accesses. It is not the bottleneck.

Publication is serialise-and-send. The public feed cannot be sent before the private acknowledgement in some designs and must not be sent to the exchange's own customers before the SIP in all designs, which is a rule about fairness rather than performance.

7.5 The Two Deliberate Delays

Two published delays exist in US equity matching, and both are regulatory artifacts rather than engineering failures.

IEX's 350 microseconds, described in Section 5.4, is imposed on every inbound order and cancellation but not on the exchange's own market data intake. It exists so that the exchange can update its pegged order prices before acting on an order that arrived during a price move.

The SIP's consolidation latency, described in Section 11, is the other. The CTA SIP's processor latency, measured from the network entrance of the CTS/CQS environment to the network exit, averaged 19.9 microseconds on the quote feed and 20.9 microseconds on the trade feed in July 2026. That is the processing cost alone and excludes the geographic round trip to the processor, which is the part that matters.

8.2 What the Numbers Actually Are

Follow the arithmetic through and the design decisions become obvious.

The dark fill. 500 shares at 190.035. The displayed offer was 190.04 and the displayed bid was 190.03. Both sides captured half a cent, worth 2.50 dollars on 500 shares. It is trivial per fill and it is the entire business case for midpoint dark pools, because at 17.4 billion shares a day half a cent is 87 million dollars of spread.

The lit sweep. 1,200 shares at 190.04, split across two resting orders of 700 and 500. Under price-time the 700-share order was earlier and filled first. The market impact. After the sweep, the 190.04 level is empty and the best offer advances to 190.05. The fund has bought 1,700 shares out of 50,000 and has already moved the offer one cent. The passive repost. Child order 2 posts 1,500 shares but displays only 200. The ITCH feed carries an Add Order message for 200 shares at 190.03. The fee. Nasdaq's protected quotes are subject to the Rule 610(c) access fee cap. The aggressive 1,200-share execution pays a take fee; the passive 1,500-share order, if it executes, earns a rebate. 8.3 The Part That Is Invisible

The parent order was 50,000 shares. Not one venue ever saw a 50,000-share order. The largest single instruction that reached any matching engine was 2,000 shares, and the largest displayed quantity was 200.

This is the central fact about modern equity market structure and it is almost never stated plainly. The order book does not show the demand. It shows the small fraction of demand that a router has decided to reveal in the next few seconds. Displayed depth is an output of execution strategy, not an input to it.

Everything downstream follows from that. Auctions matter because they are the one moment when hidden demand is required to declare itself. Dark pools exist because declaring is expensive. And the flash crash happened because on one afternoon in 2010, the undeclared demand turned out not to exist at all.

9. Auctions: Open, Close, Halt, and the Imbalance Feed

9.1 Why a Continuous Book Cannot Open Itself

A continuous limit order book has no way to start. At 09:29:59 there is no price, because the last price was set sixteen and a half hours ago and every piece of overnight news has arrived since. If the market simply switched on, the first order to arrive would trade against whatever stale interest happened to be resting, and the opening print would be an accident.

An auction solves it by refusing to trade until everything has arrived, then executing everything at one price. A call auction is a batch, and a batch has no queue and no speed advantage.

9.2 The Price Determination Algorithm

Nasdaq publishes the algorithm in three ordered steps, applied to all orders on the Nasdaq book plus the inside prices at the time of the cross:

  1. Maximise the number of shares executed.
  2. Minimise the imbalance of cross orders.
  3. Minimise the distance from the Nasdaq inside bid-ask midpoint.

The steps are lexicographic. Step 2 breaks ties left by step 1; step 3 breaks ties left by step 2.

9.3 The Imbalance Feed

The auction's second function is to publish, in advance, what it is about to do. This is the Net Order Imbalance Indicator, and it is the mechanism that pulls offsetting liquidity in before the auction runs.

Nasdaq's schedule is precise. NOII dissemination starts at 09:25 for the opening cross and 15:50 for the closing cross. Between 09:25 and 09:28, and between 15:50 and 15:55, it publishes every 10 seconds. Between 09:28 and 09:30, and between 15:55 and 16:00, it publishes every second. For halts, IPOs, and pauses, NOII goes out at one-second intervals starting one second after the quoting period begins. An Extended Trading Close message is disseminated from 16:00 to 16:05 at five-second intervals.

The Price Variation Indicator is a single byte that buckets how far the near indicative clearing price sits from the reference price: L under 1%, 1 through 9 for each whole percentage band up to 9.99%, A for 10 to 19.99%, B for 20 to 29.99%, C for 30% or more, space for not calculable. It exists so a subscriber can filter for auctions about to print far from the continuous market without decoding and comparing two prices.

9.4 The Order Types That Only Exist for Auctions

Market-on-open and market-on-close execute at the auction price whatever it is. Nasdaq's MOO cutoff is 09:28; MOC is 15:55. Orders after those cutoffs are not accepted.

Limit-on-open and limit-on-close cap the price. Nasdaq accepts LOO until 09:29:30, but an LOO entered after 09:28 is accepted at its limit price unless that limit is more aggressive than the 09:28 price or the previous day's official closing price, in which case it is repriced to the more aggressive of the two. LOC orders must be received before 15:58, and an LOC after 15:55 is repriced to the more aggressive of the 15:50 and 15:55 reference prices. An LOO with a non-IOC time in force received after 09:29:30 is converted into an imbalance-only order.

Imbalance-only orders are the interesting design. They are limit orders that can only offset an imbalance and can never add to one. Buy and sell imbalance-only orders are repriced to the Nasdaq best bid and best offer respectively before the cross, and after repricing they are treated as ordinary limit orders at that price with a timestamp equal to their original entry time. They may be submitted but not updated or cancelled. They count toward paired shares.

The purpose is to let a participant say "I will supply liquidity into whatever imbalance exists, at the inside, and I will not make the imbalance worse." Without that instruction, a participant wanting to offset a closing imbalance would have to guess the direction in advance.

The NYSE's auctions are on a different schedule and a different design, because a human is involved.

9.6 Why the Close Is the Most Important Minute of the Day

The closing auction produces the official closing price, and the official closing price is a contractual object. Index funds must match an index whose level is computed from official closes. Mutual fund net asset values are struck on official closes. Derivatives settle on them. Performance is measured against them.

A fund tracking an index therefore cannot trade at 15:47 and call it done. It must trade at the close, at the closing price, or it takes tracking error. That obligation concentrates a large, price-insensitive, mechanically predictable quantity of volume into a single batch, which is why the imbalance feed exists and why the ten minutes before 16:00 are the most heavily surveilled window in the trading day.

The auction has one more property that nothing else in the market has. It is a batch, so speed is worthless inside it. A firm with a 400-nanosecond network advantage gains nothing at 16:00:00 that it did not already have at 15:59:00. The close is the one part of US equity market structure where latency does not pay.

10. NYSE's DMM Model Against Nasdaq's Pure Electronic Book

10.1 Two Answers to One Question

Both exchanges answer the same question: who guarantees that a stock can be traded when nobody wants to trade it. Nasdaq's answer is nobody in particular, and competition among many market makers will supply it. The NYSE's answer is one named firm with filed obligations.

10.2 What a DMM Actually Does

The NYSE describes the role in one sentence: DMMs have obligations to maintain fair and orderly markets for their assigned securities, and they operate both manually and electronically to facilitate price discovery during market opens, closes, and periods of trading imbalance or instability.

Unpack the three clauses.

"Both manually and electronically" means the DMM runs an algorithm that quotes continuously and also has a human who can override it. Every other US venue is one of those two things.

"Facilitate price discovery during market opens" is a scheduling fact with consequences. NYSE orders queue from 06:30 and can be entered and cancelled until the security is opened by the DMM, which may occur after 09:30. A NYSE-listed security does not necessarily open at 09:30. It opens when its DMM opens it.

"Periods of trading imbalance or instability" is the part that only exists at the NYSE. When a closing imbalance is large, the DMM commits capital to absorb it.

10.3 Three Firms

There is exactly one DMM per NYSE-listed security, and in 2026 three firms hold all of them: Citadel Securities, Virtu Americas, and GTS Securities.

That number is the argument against the model in one line. The obligation is filed and enforceable, but the population of firms willing to accept it has consolidated to three, and two of those three are also the largest retail wholesalers in the country. The firm that is the accountable liquidity provider of last resort in a NYSE listing is frequently the same firm internalising the retail flow in that listing off-exchange.

Whether that is a conflict or an efficiency depends on the filing.

10.4 The Supplemental Liquidity Provider

Between the DMM and the ordinary book, the NYSE inserted a third category. Supplemental Liquidity Providers are electronic, high-volume members incentivised to add liquidity. They trade only proprietarily, never for public customers and never on an agency basis. They must maintain a bid or offer at the NBBO in each assigned security at least 10 percent of the trading day. When their posted liquidity executes against incoming orders they receive a rebate. All NYSE stocks are eligible, but SLPs concentrate in names trading more than a million shares a day.

The approved list in 2026 is GTS, Goldman Sachs, HRT Financial, IMC Chicago, Jane Street, Jump Trading, Latour Trading, Pundion, and Virtu on the proprietary side, with Citadel Securities, Goldman Sachs, and Virtu registered as supplemental market makers.

The SLP is a contract: quote at the inside a tenth of the time, get paid when the quote is hit. It buys quoted presence rather than committed capital, which is a cheaper obligation than the DMM's and a weaker one.

10.5 Which Design Wins

Neither, and the market share data says so. Nasdaq traded 14.27% of US share volume in July 2026 and the NYSE traded 8.34%, but NYSE Arca, a pure electronic price-time book operated by the same company, traded 9.76%. The NYSE's parent runs both models, and the electronic one carries more volume than the floor one.

The honest summary is narrower than the usual argument. The DMM model has never been shown to produce better continuous-market quality than a well-run electronic book. What it produces is an accountable party at the open, the close, and during a halt reopening, which are exactly the three moments when a continuous book has no price and needs someone to make one.

Nasdaq solves the same three moments with a published algorithm. The NYSE solves them with a published algorithm and a person who can override it. In March 2020, when the NYSE closed its trading floor from 23 March while the market stayed open, its listings continued to open and close electronically. The floor had closed before, for four sessions after 11 September 2001 and two for Hurricane Sandy in October 2012, but the market closed with it. This time it did not. The floor is not load-bearing. The obligation is.

11. Market Data: Proprietary Depth Feeds, the SIP, and the Latency Gap

11.1 Two Feeds, Two Purposes

Every US exchange publishes the same underlying events twice, to two different destinations, under two different legal regimes.

The proprietary depth feed carries the exchange's full order book, order by order, to subscribers who pay for it and connect directly. Nasdaq TotalView-ITCH is the canonical example. It is order-level, it includes non-displayed executions as Trade messages, it includes the imbalance indicator, and it is sold with a co-location cross-connect.

The consolidated feed carries top-of-book quotes and every trade from every venue to a single processor, which merges them, computes the NBBO, computes the LULD price bands, and republishes. There are two processors: CTA/CQ for Tapes A and B, operated by the Securities Industry Automation Corporation, with the NYSE administering Network A and NYSE American administering Network B, and UTP for Tape C, operated by Nasdaq.

The exchange must send the SIP the data, and Regulation NMS forbids it from sending its own customers the data any sooner. The SEC and CFTC's flash crash report states the rule and its limit in the same paragraph: proprietary feeds "cannot be sent to customers any sooner than the data provided to the processors. However, because the proprietary data feeds are not consolidated, such data feeds may reach the end user faster than the consolidated feeds."

Simultaneous departure, different arrival. That sentence is the whole controversy.

11.2 What the Gap Actually Is

The gap between a direct feed and the SIP has three components, and only one of them is published.

Consolidation cost. The processor must receive from every venue, merge, and recompute. The CTA SIP publishes this monthly. In July 2026 the CQS quote feed averaged 19.9 microseconds with a median of 18.0 and a 99th percentile of 24.5, and the CTS trade feed averaged 20.9 microseconds with a median of 18.6 and a 99th percentile of 31.8. The measurement runs from the moment an inbound participant message reaches the network entrance of the CTS/CQS environment to the moment the outbound consolidated message reaches the network exit.

Two geographic hops. The exchange's message must travel to the processor, and the consolidated message must travel back out to the subscriber. A direct feed subscriber co-located in the same building as the matching engine skips both. This component is not published by either SIP and depends entirely on where the subscriber sits.

Aggregation semantics. The SIP publishes only the best bid and offer per exchange. The flash crash report is explicit: "The CQS provides only the best bid and best offer per exchange. Bids and offers deeper in each exchange's books are not disseminated through CQS." A direct feed subscriber sees the whole book. A SIP subscriber sees twenty top-of-book quotes.

Twenty microseconds of processing is not the problem. The other two are.

11.4 The Structural Objection

The SIP is the official price and it is not the fastest price. That is the objection, stated in full.

Every consequence follows from it. Best execution is measured against the NBBO, which is computed by the SIP. Rule 611 protects quotes identified by the SIP. LULD price bands are computed by the SIPs and republished every 30 seconds. Every regulatory obligation in US equities references a data product that arrives after the data product used to make the trading decision.

Firms that can afford direct feeds from every venue build their own consolidated view, which is faster and more complete than the official one. Firms that cannot use the SIP. The 2024 Reg NMS amendments attack one dimension of this, by requiring odd-lot quotes and a best odd-lot order on the SIP, but not the timing dimension.

The counterargument, made consistently by the exchanges, is that the SIP is 20 microseconds of processing and the rest is the subscriber's own network choice, which the exchange does not control and cannot fix. Both statements are true. They do not resolve the objection.

11.5 What the SIPs Are Adding

The consolidated feeds are in the middle of the largest set of concurrent changes in their history. The CTA and UTP Operating Committees listed them in an August 2025 filing: incorporation of fractional share trading data, implementation of the amended round lot definition on or before 3 November 2025, a potential transition toward 24-hour SIP operations, the possible move to half-cent tick sizes, and infrastructure expansion for growing message traffic.

On the odd-lot requirement the committees asked the SEC for a two-year exemption, to May 2028, from disseminating odd-lot quotes beyond each participant's best odd-lot bid and offer. Under their proposal, from May 2026 the SIPs would disseminate top-of-book odd-lot quotations priced at or better than the NBBO: the best odd-lot bid and offer across all participants, known as the BOLO, plus the best odd-lot bid and offer from each participant. The committees told the industry to keep building for full depth of book because they did not know whether the exemption would be granted.

The SIP participant list itself has grown. As of 2026 the CTA plan lists 21 participants, including 24X National Exchange, Nasdaq TEX, NYSE Texas, and the Texas Stock Exchange alongside the established venues.

12. Regulation NMS: the Order Protection Rule, the NBBO, and Access Fees

12.1 What Rule 611 Says

Rule 611(a)(1) requires a trading center to establish, maintain, and enforce written policies and procedures reasonably designed to prevent trade-throughs on that trading center of protected quotations in NMS stocks that do not fall within an exception, and, when relying on an exception, to assure compliance with its terms. Rule 611(a)(2) requires the trading center to surveil the effectiveness of those policies and remedy deficiencies promptly.

Read that carefully, because it does not say what people think it says. It is a policies-and-procedures rule, not a per-trade prohibition. A single trade-through is not automatically a violation; a system without reasonable policies is.

A trade-through is an execution at a price inferior to a protected quotation displayed elsewhere. A protected quotation must be an automated quotation, defined in Rule 600(b)(6) as one displayed by a trading center that permits an incoming order to be marked immediate-or-cancel, immediately and automatically executes such an order against the displayed quotation up to its full size, and immediately and automatically cancels any unexecuted portion.

That definition is why the manual floor quote lost its protection in 2005 and why the NYSE was forced to become a hybrid market. If a quote cannot be hit electronically and instantly, it is not protected, and the rest of the market may trade through it.

12.2 The Exceptions Are the Rule

Nine exceptions sit in Rule 611(b), and three of them define modern market structure.

Exception (b)(3), the auction exception. A single-priced opening, reopening, or closing transaction is exempt. Without it, no auction could print at all, because a batch clearing at one price will necessarily trade through some venue's quote for some participants.

Exceptions (b)(5) and (b)(6), the ISO exceptions. These are what make large orders executable. (b)(5) says a venue may execute an order marked ISO without regard to protected quotes elsewhere. (b)(6) says a venue may trade through if it simultaneously routes ISOs to execute against the full displayed size of every protected quote it is about to trade through. The SEC's own explanation of the design: the exception is consistent with protecting the best displayed prices "because it is premised on the condition that the trading center or broker-dealer responsible for routing the order will have attempted to access all better-priced protected quotations up to their displayed size."

Exception (b)(8), the one-second exception. If the venue displaying the protected quote had, within one second before the execution, displayed a quote equal to or inferior to the trade-through price, the trade-through is excused. One second is a geological era in a market measured in nanoseconds. The exception exists because quotes flicker, and enforcing protection against a quote that existed for 200 microseconds would be unadministrable.

12.3 What Rule 611 Does Not Protect

The Order Protection Rule protects the top of each venue's book only, and only its displayed size, and only when the quote is automated.

The second-best price at a venue is unprotected. Non-displayed liquidity is unprotected. Reserve quantity behind a displayed iceberg is unprotected. An odd lot is unprotected, and the November 2025 round lot change did not alter that. What changed is the boundary. In a 900-dollar stock the round lot was 100 shares, so a 50-share order priced better than the NBBO was an odd lot and invisible to the protection regime; the round lot in that stock is now 40 shares, so the same order is a round lot and is protected as one. The category still gets nothing.

And the rule is not a best execution rule. Best execution is a separate obligation owed by a broker to its customer under FINRA rules and SEC guidance. Rule 611 constrains venues; best execution constrains brokers. A broker can comply with Rule 611 perfectly and still execute badly.

12.4 Rule 610 and the Access Fee Cap

Rule 610 governs access. Its operative economic provision is paragraph (c), which caps what a trading center may charge to execute against its own protected quotation or its own best bid or offer.

As adopted in 2005 the cap was a flat 0.003 dollars per share, 30 mils, and the Commission's stated purpose was that an investor accessing a 10.00-dollar offer would pay no more than 10.00 dollars plus 0.003 dollars. The 2024 amendments reduce it. Rule 610(c) as amended reads: if the price of the quotation is 1.00 dollar or more, the fee or fees cannot exceed or accumulate to more than 0.001 dollars per share, which is 10 mils; if the price is less than 1.00 dollar, the cap is 0.1 percent of the quotation price per share.

The amendments also add a new Rule 610(d): a national securities exchange shall not impose any fee, nor provide any rebate or other remuneration, for the execution of an order in an NMS stock that cannot be determined at the time of execution. That single sentence outlaws the volume-tier pricing model that every US exchange runs, under which a member's effective rate depends on how much it traded that month and is therefore unknowable when a trade prints.

Compliance status as of August 2026 is covered in the next section, and it is not what the adopting release said.

12.5 The Access Fee Cap Is What Makes the Maker-Taker Model Possible

Rule 610(c) caps the take fee. It does not cap the rebate. An exchange running a maker-taker model charges the aggressor up to the cap and pays the passive side something less, keeping the difference.

The arithmetic is tight. At a 30-mil cap, a venue charging 30 mils to take and paying 29 mils to make keeps 1 mil per share, which is 0.00001 dollars. On 17.4 billion shares a day across the whole market, a mil is 174,000 dollars. This is a volume business with a margin measured in ten-thousandths of a cent, which is why exchanges care about market share to two decimal places.

Inverted venues run the model backwards, paying the aggressor and charging the passive side, which is why Cboe BYX and Nasdaq BX exist alongside their sister markets with nearly identical technology.

The SEC's stated reason for lowering the cap is that a 30-mil fee is large relative to a half-penny spread. If the minimum tick becomes 0.005 dollars, a 0.003-dollar access fee is 60% of the spread, and the displayed price stops meaning what it appears to mean. That argument is the connective tissue between Rule 610 and Rule 612, and it is why the SEC moved both at once.

13. Tick Sizes, Round Lots, and the 2024 Amendments

13.1 The Tick Is the Unit of Competition

A tick is the minimum increment in which a price may be quoted, and it is the granularity at which time priority can be bought. If the tick is one cent, the only way to jump the queue at 190.03 is to bid 190.04 and pay a full cent. If the tick were a tenth of a cent, jumping costs a tenth as much.

Wide ticks therefore make queue position valuable and make time priority binding. Narrow ticks make price competition cheap and queue position nearly worthless. Neither is obviously better, and the entire tick size debate is an argument about which stocks should be in which regime.

Rule 612 as adopted in 2005 was simple: no display, ranking, or acceptance of any bid, offer, order, or indication of interest in an NMS stock in an increment smaller than 0.01 dollars if priced at 1.00 dollar or more, and no smaller than 0.0001 dollars if priced below 1.00 dollar.

13.2 The Problem the SEC Identified

A stock is tick-constrained when its natural spread is narrower than one cent. It cannot express that spread, so the spread sits at exactly one cent and the queue at each side becomes very long.

The SEC quantified it. Analysing NMS stocks in 2023, the Commission identified 2,420 NMS stocks with a Time Weighted Average Quoted Spread of 0.015 dollars or less, representing about 74% of share volume and about 47% of dollar volume. Earlier analysis covering January through May 2022 found 1,707 stocks with a TWAQS below 0.016 dollars, about 64% of share volume and 37.9% of dollar volume.

Three quarters of US share volume trades in stocks whose spread is pinned by the tick.

Meanwhile sub-penny pricing already existed in two places. Off-exchange wholesalers were free to provide sub-penny price improvement, because Rule 612 restricts displaying, ranking, and accepting orders, not executing them. And exchanges had obtained SEC exemptions from Rule 612 for their retail liquidity programs, letting them accept and rank certain retail quotes in increments as small as 0.001 dollars specifically so they could compete with OTC market makers.

The lit market was quoting in pennies while the dark market was executing in tenths of a penny.

13.3 What the SEC Adopted on 18 September 2024

Release 34-101070, effective 9 December 2024, rewrites Rule 612 as a two-tier variable regime.

The measurement. Time Weighted Average Quoted Spread means the average dollar difference between the National Best Bid and the National Best Offer during regular trading hours, where each unique NBB/NBO pair is weighted by the length of time it prevailed.

The evaluation periods. January through March, and July through September. The primary listing exchange measures TWAQS for each NMS stock over the period.

The assignment. For quotes priced at 1.00 dollar or more: 0.01 dollars if TWAQS was greater than 0.015 dollars; 0.005 dollars if TWAQS was equal to or less than 0.015 dollars. Below 1.00 dollar the increment stays 0.0001 dollars.

The operative dates. The increment from the January-to-March period becomes operative on the first business day of May and runs through the last business day of October. The increment from the July-to-September period becomes operative on the first business day of November and runs through the last business day of April.

New listings. Any security that becomes an NMS stock mid-period is assigned a 0.01-dollar increment.

The Commission declined to adopt the two additional tiers it had proposed at 0.002 and 0.001 dollars, and it explicitly did not adopt a minimum pricing increment for trades. Sub-penny execution remains legal. Only quoting is constrained.

13.4 The Round Lot Rewrite

The second change is less discussed, larger, and older than the rest of this section. The price-tiered round lot definition was adopted in the Market Data Infrastructure rules, Release 34-90610 of 9 December 2020, published at 86 FR 18596. The 2024 amendments did not create the tiers. They changed tier assignment from monthly to semiannual and accelerated implementation to November 2025. Before that, a round lot was 100 shares for every NMS stock. Rule 600(b)(93) now tiers it by the average closing price on the primary listing exchange during the prior evaluation period:

Average closing price Round lot
250.00 USD or less 100 shares
250.01 to 1,000.00 USD 40 shares
1,000.01 to 10,000.00 USD 10 shares
10,000.01 USD or more 1 share

The reason is arithmetic. In a stock trading at 3,000 dollars, a 100-share order is 300,000 dollars of notional. Almost nobody quotes that, so almost nothing in that stock qualified as a round lot, so almost nothing appeared on the SIP, so the official NBBO in the most expensive stocks in the market was largely fictional. Cutting the round lot to 10 shares makes the quote real.

The amendments also add a definition of the best odd-lot order to buy and best odd-lot order to sell: the highest-priced odd-lot buy order priced above the national best bid, and the lowest-priced odd-lot sell order priced below the national best offer, calculated and disseminated by a competing consolidator, plan processor, or self-aggregator. Where two market centers transmit identical odd-lot orders, they are ranked first by size, largest highest, and then by time, earliest highest.

And Rule 600(b)(89)(i)(F) adds to the definition of regulatory data an indicator of the applicable minimum pricing increment, so a subscriber can tell from the feed which tick regime a stock is in.

13.5 What Actually Happened: the Stay, the Court, and the Delay

This is where a reader relying on the adopting release alone gets the current state of the market wrong.

Between 18 September and 30 October 2024, petitions for review of the final rules were filed in the D.C. Circuit by We the Investors and by Cboe Global Markets and its four equity exchanges. On 3 December 2024 Nasdaq, its three exchanges, and the four Cboe equity exchanges moved the Commission to stay the effect of the amendments to Rules 610 and 612 pending resolution.

On 12 December 2024 the SEC granted a partial stay of the amendments to Rules 600(b)(89)(i)(F), 610(c), and 612, pending completion of judicial review. The Commission was careful to say it was not departing from its view that the rules are lawful, and that a stay avoided "the potential for market disruption and regulatory uncertainty" while validity was contested.

On 14 October 2025 the D.C. Circuit denied the petition for review.

On 31 October 2025 the Commission issued Release 34-104172, granting temporary exemptive relief from the compliance dates. The relief moved Rules 600(b)(89)(i)(F) and 612, the minimum pricing increment, and Rule 610(c), the access fee caps, to the first business day of November 2026; and Rule 610(d), the requirement that fees and rebates be determinable at the time of execution, to the first business day of February 2026. It also excused exchanges from filing rule changes reflecting the new round lot definition until 30 days after the end of the then-current lapse in appropriations.

Robinhood's Form 10-Q for the quarter ended 30 June 2026, filed 30 July 2026, records the next step: "On June 11, 2026, the SEC further extended the compliance dates for the minimum pricing increment and access fee cap rules to November 2027."

The current state, as of August 2026, is therefore:

Two years of legal process moved the tick change from November 2025 to November 2027. The stocks are still quoted in pennies.

13.6 Why the Exchanges Fought It

The exchanges' objection is economic and easy to state. Their transaction revenue is a per-share fee bounded by the access fee cap. Cutting the cap from 30 mils to 10 mils cuts the maximum gross take fee by two thirds. Simultaneously halving the tick lengthens nothing and shortens queues, reducing the value of the rebate that attracts posted liquidity in the first place.

The counter-position, advanced by the SEC and by retail advocacy petitioners on the other side of the same litigation, is that a 30-mil fee against a 50-mil spread distorts the displayed price by 60%, and that quoting in pennies while wholesalers execute in tenths of a penny hands off-exchange venues a structural advantage that has nothing to do with execution quality.

Both parties are describing the same number from opposite ends. The tick is the unit of competition, and whoever sets it decides who competes.

14. Payment for Order Flow and Internalisation

14.1 The Mechanism

A retail market order in the United States usually never reaches an exchange. The broker sells the right to execute it to a wholesaler, which fills it as principal from its own inventory at a price at or better than the NBBO, and reports the print to a FINRA Trade Reporting Facility.

Robinhood's own 10-Q describes the arrangement in the first person: "we receive consideration in exchange for routing our users' equity, option, and cryptocurrency trade orders to market makers, wholesalers and other liquidity providers (together, 'Liquidity Providers') for execution. With respect to equities and options trading, such fees are known as payment for order flow, or 'PFOF.'"

14.2 Why the Wholesaler Pays

The economics rest on one asymmetry: retail market orders do not predict the next price move, and orders resting on a public exchange might.

A market maker posting a bid on an exchange faces adverse selection. Some of the counterparties who hit that bid know something, and the market maker's inventory moves against it. That risk is priced into the spread the market maker is willing to quote.

A segregated stream of retail market orders carries much less of that risk. The wholesaler can therefore quote inside the public spread and still expect to profit, and it can pay the broker for the privilege of receiving the stream. The payment is a rebate of part of that expected profit.

This is not a scam and it is not free money. It is a payment for the absence of information, and it is real.

14.3 The Numbers

Robinhood is the most transparent case because it is a public company that breaks transaction-based revenue into payment for order flow, crypto transaction rebates, and event-contract fees separately.

For the three months ended 30 June 2026: total transaction-based revenue of 776 million dollars, comprising options 342 million, event contracts 156 million, cryptocurrencies 100 million, equities 129 million, and other 49 million. That was 59% of total net revenues of 1,308 million dollars. For the six months ended 30 June 2026: 1,399 million dollars of transaction-based revenue, also 59% of net revenues.

Three features of that table matter more than the totals.

Event contracts are not payment for order flow. The 156 million dollars are exchange and clearing transaction fees on Robinhood's prediction-market business, which is a fifth of the transaction-based line. Treating the whole 776 million as order-flow payments overstates them by 20%.

Equities produce a third of what options produce. Equities produced 129 million dollars against options at 342 million. Options PFOF is worth several times equity PFOF per unit of customer activity, because options spreads are wider and the per-contract payment is larger. The public argument is about stocks. The money is in options.

The arrangements are not contracts. Robinhood's risk disclosure states that its PFOF and transaction rebate arrangements with liquidity providers "are a matter of practice and business understanding and are often not documented under binding contracts (as is generally the case with Liquidity Providers in equities and options)." Half a billion dollars a quarter rests on a business understanding.

14.4 The Structural Objection

The objection is not that retail investors get bad prices. On measured metrics they usually get prices better than the displayed NBBO. The objection is about what the NBBO is.

Follow it in three steps. Uninformed retail flow is removed from the public book. The public book therefore faces a more informed mix of counterparties on average. Market makers on the public book widen their quotes to compensate. The NBBO widens. And price improvement is measured against the NBBO.

The wholesaler improves on a benchmark that the practice of internalisation helps to set. Whether the net effect on retail investors is positive is an empirical question that has been argued for a decade without resolution, in part because the counterfactual, a market where all retail flow is exposed, has not existed since before 1997.

The measurable facts are that off-exchange share volume has run between 46.54% and 52.27% of the US total every month from January 2025 through July 2026, and that the wholesaler business is highly concentrated among a handful of firms, two of which are also NYSE Designated Market Makers.

14.5 What Regulation Actually Does About It

The United States does not ban PFOF. The United Kingdom and the European Union restrict it; the US regulates disclosure and execution quality instead.

Rule 606 requires brokers to disclose their order routing, including the venues they route to and the payment received. Rule 605 requires market centers to publish monthly execution quality statistics.

Rule 605 was substantially amended on 6 March 2024, in Release 34-99679, effective 14 June 2024. The amendments expand the reporting population from market centers to broker-dealers that introduce or carry at least 100,000 customer accounts; the SEC noted such broker-dealers together handle over 98% of customer accounts. They expand covered orders to include certain orders submitted outside regular trading hours and certain orders with stop prices. They change the order size categories to capture fractional share orders, odd-lot orders, and larger-sized orders. They require statistics for non-marketable limit orders to be reported from the time the order becomes executable rather than from receipt. They require time to execution to be measured in increments of a millisecond or finer, calculated on a share-weighted basis, and realized spread to be calculated at multiple intervals. And they require every reporting entity to publish a summary report.

The compliance date was set at 18 months after the effective date and has since been extended; Robinhood's 10-Q records a compliance date of 1 August 2026 for the Rule 605 amendments.

The theory is that better measurement disciplines routing. It is a reasonable theory. It has been the American answer for twenty-five years, and off-exchange share has risen throughout.

15. Dark Pools, ATSs, and Off-Exchange Trading

15.1 The Legal Construction

A dark pool is not a legal category. The legal category is an alternative trading system: a trading system that meets the statutory definition of an exchange but is not required to register as one, because it operates under the exemption in Exchange Act Rule 3a1-1(a) and complies with Rules 300 through 303 of Regulation ATS.

To use the exemption an ATS must register as a broker-dealer and file an initial operation report on Form ATS before commencing operations, then file amendments for any change and a cessation report when it stops. Form ATS is a notice, not an application; the Commission does not approve an ATS before it begins operating.

ATSs that trade NMS stocks are held to a higher standard. Rule 304 requires them to publicly file Form ATS-N, disclosing the manner of operation of the ATS and the ATS-related activities of the broker-dealer operator and its affiliates. Unlike Form ATS, an initial Form ATS-N becomes effective only on completion of Commission review and publication, or on expiration of the review period, and the Commission may declare it ineffective after notice and an opportunity for a hearing.

The population as of mid-2026: 81 ATSs with a Form ATS on file as of 31 July 2026, covering fixed income, private markets, securities lending, and digital assets; and 34 NMS stock ATSs with an effective Form ATS-N.

15.2 The Taxonomy

Reading the ATS-N list, four business models are visible.

Broker-dealer internalisation pools. Operated by a large broker to cross its own client flow. UBS ATS, SIGMA X2 (Goldman Sachs), JPM-X and JPB-X (J.P. Morgan), Instinct X (BofA), the Barclays ATS, Citi-ONE, MS POOL and MS RPOOL and MS Trajectory Cross (Morgan Stanley), CrossStream (National Financial Services), Stifel X, BNPP Cortex.

Independent block venues. Built to find natural counterparties for institutional size without displaying it. Liquidnet Negotiation ATS and Liquidnet H2O, BIDS ATS, Instinet BlockCross, CBX, LeveL ATS, POSIT and Virtu MatchIt (Virtu Americas), PureStream.

Algorithmic and periodic-auction venues. IntelligentCross, which runs discrete matching events designed to reduce adverse selection, and OneChronos, which runs a combinatorial auction.

Extended-hours and specialist venues. BOATS, operated by Blue Ocean ATS, which trades US equities during Asian hours; MOON ATS, operated by OTC Link; and newer entrants including Admiral ATS, effective March 2026, and Mosaic ATS, effective March 2026.

15.3 What "Dark" Actually Means

A dark venue does not display quotations. That is the entire definition, and it has three precise consequences.

No pre-trade transparency. There is no bid and no offer to see. A participant sends an order and learns whether it matched.

No Rule 611 protection. A non-displayed order is not a protected quotation, so nobody is obliged to respect it. Correspondingly, an ATS may not trade through the NBBO, so it must reference the lit market it does not contribute to.

Full post-trade transparency. Every ATS execution is reported to a FINRA Trade Reporting Facility and appears on the consolidated tape within the required reporting period, with the venue identified generically rather than by name. The trade is public. Only the intention was private.

Most NMS stock ATS volume executes at the NBBO midpoint. That is the product: both sides split the spread, and neither side signalled its intent beforehand.

15.4 Why an Institution Uses One

Return to the worked example in Section 8. A 50,000-share parent order against a 1,200-share displayed offer cannot be executed on lit venues without moving the price. Every child order that takes liquidity removes a level and advertises the presence of a buyer.

A dark pool offers a different trade. Send the order, wait, and if a natural seller of similar size arrives, cross at the midpoint with no market impact at all. The cost is time and the risk is information leakage to the operator and to other participants who are probing the pool with small orders to detect large ones.

Minimum quantity instructions exist to manage exactly that risk. A 500-share order with a 100-share minimum will not be picked apart by a sequence of one-share pings.

15.5 The Aggregate Picture

Off-exchange volume is not mostly dark pools. It is mostly wholesaler internalisation.

The three FINRA Trade Reporting Facilities carried 50.16% of US share volume in July 2026, and the FINRA/Nasdaq TRF at Carteret alone carried 47.07%. That single facility is the reporting destination for retail internalisation, which is the largest component of off-exchange volume by a wide margin. The 34 NMS stock ATSs report through the same facilities and account for a much smaller share.

The distinction matters because the two activities have opposite characters. An ATS crossing institutional blocks at the midpoint is a mechanism for reducing market impact on orders too large to display. A wholesaler internalising retail market orders is a mechanism for segregating uninformed flow. Both print to the same tape and both count as "off-exchange," and conflating them produces most of the confused commentary on the subject.

16.2 Market-Wide Circuit Breakers

The mechanism is simple and its plumbing is not. At 07:00 and again at 08:00, the CTA SIP generates three decline values, 7%, 13%, and 20%, from the S&P 500's index value at 16:00 the previous trading day, and disseminates them over multicast. On ITCH they arrive as a V message carrying three Price(8) fields. Beginning at 09:30, the CTA SIP ingests SPX from a data stream provided by S&P Dow Jones Indices via Cboe. SPX values are generated every one second, and the SIP's MWCB application monitors the percentage decline.

On a 7% breach the application generates Breach Level 1 messages to CTS/CQS and to the UTP SIP, which disseminate them. On ITCH this is a W message carrying a single byte, 1, 2, or 3. Primary listing exchanges then send Regulatory Halt messages for each of their listed securities. At the end of the 15-minute halt they send Resume messages. But an exchange does not actually resume trading in a security until it receives LULD price bands for it, which in practice means until the primary listing exchange has reopened it.

A Level 1 or Level 2 breach after 09:30 and before 15:25 halts the market for 15 minutes, and each may occur only once a day. The same decline at or after 15:25 does not halt anything. A Level 3 breach at any time halts trading for the remainder of the day.

Level 3 has never been triggered.

On 16 March, the report notes, the drop was so fast that the vast majority of S&P 500 stocks had not completed a primary listing exchange opening auction before the Level 1 halt triggered. After the close on 18 March, the NYSE announced that its trading floor would close effective Monday 23 March. The listings continued to open and close.

16.4 Limit Up Limit Down

LULD is the single-stock mechanism, and unlike the market-wide breakers it is a price band rather than a halt. It prevents trades outside the band rather than stopping trading when one occurs.

The plan was filed on 5 April 2011 by the exchanges and FINRA, approved by the SEC on a pilot basis on 31 May 2012, and approved as a permanent rule on 11 April 2019. It is administered by an Operating Committee with one representative from each participating exchange and FINRA. The plan's published participant list is stale and no count should be taken from it: it still names Nasdaq BX and NYSE Chicago, both renamed before July 2026, and omits MEMX, MIAX Pearl, 24X National Exchange, and the Texas Stock Exchange, all of which reported NMS stock volume that month.

The reference price is the arithmetic mean of Eligible Reported Transactions over the preceding five minutes. If no eligible trades occurred, the previous reference price stands. The reference price is updated after 30 seconds only if the new value would be at least 1% away from the current one, and both SIPs republish the current bands every 30 seconds. "Eligible reported transactions" are only those eligible to update the last sale price, which excludes trades such as VWAP prints that qualify for a Rule 611 exemption.

The first reference price of the day is the primary listing exchange's opening price, or the midpoint of the primary's quote if it does not open within five minutes, or the previous day's closing price if the opening auction concludes with no volume.

The percentage parameters depend on tier and price, and are fixed by the prior day's primary-market close, not by the intraday price:

16.5 The Limit State, and Why 15 Seconds

An NMS stock enters a Limit State when the National Best Offer equals the lower price band without crossing the National Best Bid, or the National Best Bid equals the upper price band without crossing the National Best Offer. In other words, when the whole market has come to rest against the band.

During the Limit State the SIP freezes: no new reference prices, no new price bands. The stock may continue to trade within the band. It exits the Limit State when the entire size of all Limit State quotations is executed or cancelled, at which point the SIP recomputes the bands from a reference price that includes the Limit State period.

If it has not exited after 15 seconds, the primary listing exchange declares a five-minute Trading Pause, with a maximum of ten minutes. During the pause no trading occurs but quoting continues and new orders are accepted. The primary reopens with its standard halt cross, and publishes an LULD Auction Collar message, ITCH type J, carrying the auction collar reference price, the upper collar price, the lower collar price, and a 4-byte count of collar extensions.

The 15-second delay is the design's central compromise. A price that hits a band because of one large order should not halt the stock; a price that hits a band and stays there for 15 seconds is a genuine liquidity failure. The Straddle State handles the ambiguous case: when the National Best Bid is below the lower band while the offer is inside the band, or the offer is above the upper band while the bid is inside, the stock is not in a Limit State but the primary may declare a pause at its discretion if trading deviates from normal characteristics.

16.6 What Happens to Orders at the Band

The order handling is the part that catches implementers, and it differs by protocol on the same exchange.

Orders priced aggressively outside the bands are repriced to the band on entry, whether IOC or longer-lived, and non-IOC orders receive a new timestamp when booked and rank in standard price-time priority. Nasdaq does not offer rejection as an alternative.

For orders entered on OUCH, an incoming limit-priced order reprices on entry only once, and thereafter resting limit interest is cancelled if the bands move such that its price would fall outside them. For orders entered on RASH or FIX, the system reprices multiple times, cancelling and re-entering the order at the new band, with a new timestamp each time.

Orders priced passively outside the bands are accepted and may be included in the NBBO, but are non-executable. The SIPs flag such quotes so they are excluded from the NBBO calculation. If a booked order inside the bands falls outside them because the bands moved, it is cancelled off the book, including when the bands tighten at 09:45.

Routable orders are never routed at a price more aggressive than the band; a routable order whose limit is through the band is routed and executed as if it were priced at the band. Pegged orders whose instruction would place them outside the band are repriced to the band.

Every one of those behaviours is a filed rule, and every one of them changes a firm's queue position.

17. The May 2010 Flash Crash, Reconstructed

17.1 The Setting

On 6 May 2010 US equity indices fell about 5 to 6% in roughly four minutes, recovered most of it in the next fifteen, and left behind more than 20,000 trades across more than 300 securities that had executed at prices 60% or more away from their 14:40 values. The exchanges and FINRA cancelled all of them after the close.

The conditions before the event were already unusual. By 14:30 the S&P 500 volatility index was up 22.5% from the open, ten-year Treasury yields had fallen in a flight to quality, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average was down about 2.5%. The number of Liquidity Replenishment Points triggered on the NYSE, a mechanism that slows trading in a stock exhibiting rapid price moves, was substantially above average.

Liquidity had already thinned. Buy-side market depth in the E-Mini S&P 500 futures contract had fallen from nearly 6 billion dollars in the early morning to 2.65 billion, a 55% decline. Depth in SPY had fallen from about 275 million dollars to 220 million, a 20% decline.

17.2 The Trigger

At 14:32 a large fundamental trader, a mutual fund complex, initiated a program to sell 75,000 E-Mini S&P 500 contracts, valued at approximately 4.1 billion dollars, as a hedge to an existing equity position.

It executed the program through an automated algorithm programmed to feed orders into the June 2010 E-Mini market targeting an execution rate of 9% of the trading volume calculated over the previous minute, without regard to price or time.

Two comparisons make the decision legible. This was the largest net change in daily position of any trader in the E-Mini since 1 January 2010. Only two single-day sell programs of equal or larger size had been executed in the preceding twelve months, one of them by the same trader. On that prior occasion the trader used a combination of manual trading over the course of a day and several automated algorithms taking price, time, and volume into account, and it took more than five hours to execute the first 75,000 contracts.

On 6 May the algorithm executed in about 20 minutes.

17.3 The Feedback Loop

The mechanism that turned a large sell order into a crash is the specific thing worth understanding, and it is a control-theory failure rather than a market failure.

The algorithm's control input was volume. It targeted 9% of the trailing minute's volume. High-frequency traders and other intermediaries bought the first tranches, accumulating a net long position of about 3,300 contracts. Between 14:41 and 14:44 they aggressively sold about 2,000 contracts to reduce that position, and in doing so traded nearly 140,000 contracts, over 33% of total trading volume.

That selling raised volume. The algorithm read higher volume and increased its own rate. Which raised volume again.

The SEC and CFTC state the failure in one sentence: "especially in times of significant volatility, high trading volume is not necessarily a reliable indicator of market liquidity." Volume was not measuring demand. It was measuring inventory being passed between intermediaries.

Between 14:45:13 and 14:45:27, HFTs traded over 27,000 contracts, about 49% of total volume, while buying only about 200 contracts net. Fourteen seconds, 27,000 contracts, no net absorption. The report calls it a hot-potato effect.

Between 14:32 and 14:45 the Sell Algorithm sold about 35,000 of its 75,000 contracts, roughly 1.9 billion dollars. Over the same window all fundamental sellers combined sold more than 80,000 contracts net while all fundamental buyers bought only about 50,000 net, a net fundamental imbalance of 30,000 contracts. Net selling by fundamental sellers ran about 15 times the level of the same 13-minute interval on the previous three days; net buying by fundamental buyers ran about 10 times.

By 14:45:28 fewer than 1,050 contracts of buy-side resting orders remained in the E-Mini, under 1% of the morning's depth. E-Mini buy-side depth had fallen to about 58 million dollars. Prices dropped a further 1.7% in 15 seconds to an intraday low of 1056.

17.4 The Pause That Worked

At 14:45:28 the Chicago Mercantile Exchange's Stop Logic Functionality triggered and paused E-Mini trading for five seconds.

In those five seconds sell pressure eased and buy-side interest increased. Trading resumed at 14:45:33, prices stabilised, and the E-Mini began to recover, followed by SPY. The Sell Algorithm continued executing until about 14:51, into a rising market.

Five seconds. The SEC and CFTC drew the conclusion directly: pausing a market "can be an effective way of providing time for market participants to reassess their strategies, for algorithms to reset their parameters, and for an orderly market to be re-established."

Every single-stock volatility control in US equities descends from that observation.

17.5 The Second Crisis, in Individual Stocks

The E-Mini stabilised at 14:45:33. Individual equities did not, and the second crisis is the one that produced the memorable prices.

Liquidity providers had built their own pauses, designed to stop automated systems from trading when prices move beyond predefined thresholds. Those pauses fired. Once paused, each firm had to make a human assessment, and the report lists what they weighed: whether the price moves were an artifact of erroneous data, the impact on risk and position limits, intraday profit and loss, the possibility that trades would be broken leaving the firm inadvertently long or short, and whether their systems could handle a nearly tenfold increase in volume. A number of participants said that because prices fell simultaneously across many types of securities, they feared a cataclysmic event they had not yet learned about.

Some widened their spreads. Some reduced size. A significant number withdrew completely. Some fell back to manual trading and could cover only a subset of securities.

At the same time, OTC market makers who normally internalise a significant fraction of retail buy and sell orders as principal "began routing most, if not all, of these orders directly to the public exchanges where they competed with other orders for immediately available, but dwindling, liquidity." The internalisers stopped internalising and dumped retail flow onto exchanges at the exact moment the exchanges had nothing to fill it with.

Sell orders in individual securities and ETFs, including many retail stop-loss orders triggered by the falling prices, then found no buying interest.

17.6 Stub Quotes

What they found instead were stub quotes.

Exchange rules generally require a registered market maker to maintain a continuous two-sided quotation. Those rules do not generally dictate the prices at which the market maker must quote. When a market maker's liquidity is exhausted, or it is unwilling to provide liquidity, it may on some markets submit a stub quote: an offer to buy or sell at a price so far from the prevailing market that it is not intended to execute, such as a bid of one cent or an offer at 100,000 dollars, purely to satisfy the continuous quoting obligation. Some equities exchanges automatically generated stub quotes, and a subset required their market makers to use that mechanism to ensure compliance.

A market order arriving at a book that contains nothing but a stub quote executes against the stub quote. That is not a malfunction. That is the book doing exactly what it is designed to do, on a book that had been emptied of everything real.

Accenture is the clearest case. Its bids fell from about 30 dollars at 14:47:47 to 0.01 dollars by 14:47:54, a seven-second collapse. It had 112 broken trades, all occurring between 14:47:51 and 14:48:01. Ten seconds. In other securities, executions printed at 100,000 dollars against stub offers.

17.7 The Aggregate Damage, and What It Was Not

The scale is smaller than the memory of it. Between 14:40 and 15:00 approximately 2 billion shares traded with a total value exceeding 56 billion dollars, and over 98% of all shares executed at prices within 10% of their 14:40 value.

The damage was concentrated. Over 20,000 trades across more than 300 separate securities, including many ETFs, executed at prices 60% or more away from their 14:40 prices. After the close, the exchanges and FINRA met and jointly agreed to cancel all of them under their clearly erroneous trade rules.

Participants told the regulators that the 60% threshold and the opaque process for applying it were themselves a problem, because uncertainty about which trades would be broken changed their willingness to provide liquidity during the event.

Three things the flash crash was not:

Not caused by a spoofer. A separate criminal prosecution of a trader for spoofing the E-Mini market followed years later. The SEC and CFTC report attributes the event to the interaction of a price-insensitive execution algorithm with automated liquidity provision under already-thin conditions.

Not caused by market data delays. The NYSE's CQS delays that afternoon were real and severe, exceeding 10 seconds on average for 1,665 symbols in the worst window. The report's finding is that they were not a dominant factor, because firms making automated decisions used direct feeds. Their contribution was indirect: firms using consolidated data for integrity checks saw discrepancies, and those discrepancies contributed to the decision to stop.

Not stopped by market-wide circuit breakers. None triggered. The DJIA's intraday decline did not reach the thresholds then in force, and the thresholds then in force were calibrated on point moves over long windows. The report notes that the staffs were working with the markets to consider recalibrating them.

17.8 What It Produced

The regulatory response arrived in three waves and every element of Section 16 comes from it.

Single-stock circuit breakers, 2010. A pilot pausing trading market-wide in a security for five minutes if it moved 10% over the preceding five minutes. The SEC approved it for S&P 500 securities on 10 June 2010 and expanded it to the Russell 1000 and certain ETFs on 10 September 2010, on a pilot basis through 10 December 2010.

Objective clearly-erroneous standards, 2010. Approved 10 September 2010, replacing the ad hoc 60% threshold with published thresholds that vary by stock price, so participants can predict in advance which trades will be broken.

Limit Up Limit Down, 2011 to 2013. Filed 5 April 2011, approved on a pilot basis 31 May 2012, rolled out from 8 April 2013, made permanent 11 April 2019. LULD replaced the single-stock circuit breakers with a price band, on the reasoning stated in the original report: a limit up/limit down procedure "would directly prevent trades outside of specified parameters, while allowing trading to continue within those parameters," preventing many anomalous trades from ever occurring rather than only stopping the market after they had.

Stub quotes were separately banned. A market maker can no longer satisfy a continuous quoting obligation with a penny bid, and the LULD bands would make such a quote non-executable in any case.

The single most durable lesson is the CME's five seconds. The market did not need a rescue. It needed a pause long enough for automated systems to notice they were operating on a market that no longer existed.

18. Economics: What It Costs to Run an Exchange and Who Pays

18.1 Three Revenue Lines

An equity exchange sells three products, and their relative sizes are the opposite of what the public discussion assumes.

Transactions. A per-share fee on the aggressive side, capped by Rule 610(c) at 0.3 cents per share for protected quotes priced at 1.00 dollar or more, with a rebate paid to the passive side. The net capture is the difference, measured in mils.

Market data. Proprietary depth feeds sold by subscription, plus a share of the consolidated tape revenue allocated among SIP participants under the plan formulas. Data is sold per feed, per user, per redistribution right, and per device, and the pricing is filed with the SEC.

Connectivity and co-location. Cross-connects, cabinet space, power, and port fees inside the exchange's data centre. Physically the smallest product and the highest margin.

The economics are counterintuitive because transaction revenue is nearly a pass-through. An exchange charging 30 mils to take and paying 29 mils to make keeps 1 mil, or 0.00001 dollars a share. Across the whole US market's 17.4 billion daily shares, one mil is 174,000 dollars. Exchanges are not primarily trading businesses. They are data and infrastructure businesses whose trading operation exists to create the data.

18.2 The Maker-Taker Model and Why Rule 610(d) Attacks It

Under maker-taker, the aggressor pays and the passive side is paid. The purpose is to buy displayed liquidity: a market maker will post a quote it would not otherwise post because the rebate compensates for the adverse selection it faces.

Under taker-maker, or inverted pricing, the signs flip. Cboe BYX runs an inverted book alongside Cboe BZX on the same technology, because some routers value certainty of execution more than the fee, and inverted venues attract aggressive flow.

Both models depend on a pricing feature that the 2024 amendments outlaw. Exchange fee schedules are tiered by monthly volume: a member's effective rate for a trade printed on the third of the month is not known until the thirtieth. New Rule 610(d) provides that a national securities exchange shall not impose any fee, nor provide any rebate or other remuneration, for the execution of an order in an NMS stock that cannot be determined at the time of execution. That compliance date arrived on the first business day of February 2026 and was not extended.

The effect is narrow and structural. Volume tiers can survive only in a form that resolves at execution time, not retrospectively. The rebate a broker earns must be knowable when the fill occurs, which is the precondition for a customer being able to check whether their broker routed to the venue that was best for the customer or the venue that was best for the broker.

18.3 What the Buy Side Pays

Follow the total cost of a 50,000-share institutional order and the exchange fee is not the largest line.

Commission to the executing broker, negotiated, typically a fraction of a cent per share for pure execution.

Exchange fees, up to 0.3 cents per share on the aggressive portion, partially offset by rebates on the passive portion. A well-designed algorithm posts more than it takes and can run close to fee-neutral.

The spread, half of which is conceded on every aggressive fill and captured on every passive fill. At a one-cent spread this is 0.5 cents per share, larger than the exchange fee.

Market impact, which is the price the order moves against itself. On an order 42 times the displayed size, this dominates everything else and is the reason the algorithm and the dark pool exist.

Market data, paid separately, per user, per feed, all year.

The exchange fee is visible and small. The impact cost is invisible and large. Institutions optimise the second and complain about the first.

18.4 What Retail Pays

Retail pays zero commission and a spread, and the spread is paid to a wholesaler rather than to an exchange.

The full circuit: the customer pays no commission; the broker receives payment for order flow; the wholesaler executes at or inside the NBBO and keeps the residual spread; the exchange receives nothing, because the trade printed to a TRF.

Robinhood's Q2 2026 figures put a size on the retail side of the loop: 776 million dollars of transaction-based revenue in three months, 59% of total net revenues, of which equities were 129 million and options 342 million.

The exchange supplied the NBBO that priced the trade and received no part of it. That is the structural complaint the exchanges make about internalisation, and it is accurate as far as it goes.

18.5 The Market Data Fight

Market data pricing has been litigated continuously for fifteen years, and the argument is about a joint-cost allocation with no correct answer.

The exchanges' position: a proprietary depth feed is a product built at expense, sold at a filed price, in competition with fifteen other venues' feeds, and nobody is obliged to buy it.

The buy side's position: a firm cannot comply with best execution or Rule 611 without seeing the market, cannot see the market from the SIP alone because the SIP carries only top of book, and therefore must buy from every venue at whatever each charges. Demand is regulatorily compelled and the seller is a monopolist in its own book.

The SIPs publish their own metrics quarterly, and the operating data is not the pressure point. The CTA SIP recorded 100.00% system availability in every quarter from 1Q24 through 2Q26 except 2Q24 at 99.998% and 3Q24 at 99.999%, against a service level guideline of 99.98%, with a stated 10-minute recovery time for full system failures.

The system works. The argument is about what it costs and who is required to buy the alternative.

19. Security, Resilience, and Operational Risk

19.1 The Threat Model Is Not What It Looks Like

Exchanges are attractive targets and are rarely attacked in the way the public imagines. The realistic threat model has four entries and only one of them is an adversary.

Erroneous order flow from a member. A member's algorithm malfunctions and sends orders that the member did not intend. This is the highest-frequency serious incident in the industry.

Capacity exhaustion. Message rates exceed what a gateway, a sequencer, or a publishing path can absorb, and latency grows until the system is effectively unavailable. The 6 May 2010 NYSE data delays were a capacity failure, occurring while systems were mid-upgrade.

Correlated withdrawal. Every liquidity provider's automated risk control fires at once, for the same reason, and the book empties. This is not a technical failure at all and no amount of hardening prevents it, which is why the answer is a mandatory pause rather than a mandatory quote.

Deliberate manipulation. Spoofing, layering, marking the close, and wash trading. These are surveillance and enforcement problems rather than infrastructure problems.

19.2 The Controls That Actually Exist

SEC Rule 15c3-5, the Market Access Rule. A broker-dealer with market access, or providing it to customers, must maintain risk management controls reasonably designed to manage the financial, regulatory, and other risks. In practice this means pre-trade credit and capital thresholds, price collars, and order and message rate limits applied on every order before it reaches the matching engine. It is the single most important operational control in the market and it sits on the latency-critical path, which is why gateway engineering is expensive.

Kill switches and mass cancel. OUCH provides Type C Mass Cancel Request, Type D Disable Order Entry Request, and Type E Enable Order Entry Request. A member whose algorithm is misbehaving can disable its own port. The exchange can also disable it.

Clearly erroneous execution rules. After 6 May 2010 these moved from discretionary to objective, with published thresholds that vary by stock price. On ITCH a break arrives as a B Broken Trade message carrying only the Match Number of the execution being broken; on OUCH it is a Type B Broken Trade with a reason code: E erroneous, C both parties consented, S supervisory, X broken by an external third party. A trade break is final and cannot be reinstated.

Operational halt messaging. ITCH type h signals that a specific market centre has an interruption of service in a security, distinct from a regulatory trading action, because the ability to trade the security elsewhere is not affected. It carries the market code, Q Nasdaq, B BX, X PSX, and the action, H halted or T resumed.

Self-help. When one venue's systems fail or materially delay, other venues may declare self-help against it and stop routing, and Rule 611(b)(1) excepts trade-throughs of that venue's quote. Nasdaq declared self-help against NYSE Arca on 6 May 2010.

19.3 Determinism as a Safety Property

The property that makes an exchange auditable is the same property that makes it recoverable. Because the matching engine is a pure function of a sequenced input log, the state after any incident can be reconstructed exactly, a hot standby can be kept bit-identical by consuming the same log, and a disputed fill can be resolved by replay rather than by argument.

This is why exchanges do not use concurrent matching, do not use non-deterministic data structures on the critical path, and do not use anything whose behaviour depends on wall-clock timing. It also constrains every performance decision: an optimisation that introduces non-determinism is not available at any speed.

19.4 Geographic Concentration Is the Unaddressed Risk

Almost all US equity matching happens in a small number of buildings in northern New Jersey. Nasdaq matches in Carteret. NYSE matches in Mahwah. Cboe, MEMX, MIAX Pearl and others are concentrated in Secaucus. The SIPs sit alongside them.

Every exchange has a disaster recovery site and tests it. What no participant has is an independent path: a firm's co-located servers, cross-connects, and market data handlers are all in the same buildings, and a regional event affects the exchange and its members simultaneously. Industry-wide disaster recovery testing is annual and coordinated through SIFMA.

The concentration is a consequence of physics. Latency-sensitive participants must be near the matching engine, and matching engines must be near each other so that arbitrage between them is possible. Geographic dispersion would degrade every performance property the market is built on. Nobody has proposed a design that resolves the tension.

20.2 The Consolidated Tape Is the Real Difference

The United States decided in the 1970s that there would be one official price, computed centrally, and built two processors to compute it. Europe made the opposite choice at the equivalent moment: MiFID, applying from 2007, opened competition among venues without mandating a consolidated tape, on the theory that commercial data vendors would supply one.

They did not, at least not in a form that anyone accepted as official. The European Union has been trying to legislate a consolidated tape into existence ever since, most recently in the 2024 MiFIR review.

The American design has the defect described in Section 11: the official price is not the fastest price. The European design has a different defect: there is no official price at all, so a best execution obligation must be assessed against a market view that each firm assembles for itself.

Neither approach has produced a market in which every participant sees the same thing at the same time. The US at least agrees on what the thing is.

20.3 What the Absence of Rule 611 Changes

Europe has no order protection rule. A venue in the EU or UK may execute at a price worse than another venue's best quote, and nothing in the venue's rulebook forbids it. The constraint is at the firm level: a broker owes its client best execution and must be able to demonstrate the routing decision.

Two consequences follow, and both are visible in the data.

Venue competition in Europe is on execution quality and cost rather than on quote protection, because a new venue's quote is not automatically protected the day it opens. Entry is correspondingly harder, and the primary listing venues have retained larger shares than their US counterparts.

And the compliance burden sits in a different place. In the US it sits on the venue, which must maintain policies and procedures under Rule 611 and surveil their effectiveness. In Europe it sits on the broker, which must justify each routing decision. The US regulates the plumbing; Europe regulates the plumber.

20.4 Concentration Works Differently

The Tokyo Stock Exchange and HKEX are concentrated markets, and concentration removes most of the problems this document describes. With one venue there is no fragmentation, no consolidation latency, no trade-through question, no NBBO computed by a third party, and no need for intermarket sweep orders.

What concentration removes with it is competitive pressure on fees and on innovation, and a fallback when the single venue fails. The tradeoff is exactly the one the United States made in the opposite direction in 2005 and has been managing ever since.

Both structures work. The American one produces tighter spreads, more venues, more rules, and a permanent argument about market data. The Asian one produces simpler plumbing and a single point of failure.

21. Modern Developments

21.1 New Exchanges, and Where They Came From

The most concrete recent change is that the list of US equity exchanges got longer, and the new entrants are geographically motivated rather than technologically motivated.

NYSE Texas appears in the volume data from January 2025, trading 1.68 billion shares in July 2026, 0.44% of the market. It is the former NYSE Chicago, relocated and rebranded.

Nasdaq Texas, listed in the CTA plan as Nasdaq TEX, Inc., first appears under that name in January 2026, reporting under the same participant code B that previously identified Nasdaq BX.

24X National Exchange first appears in October 2025, trading 0.15 billion shares in July 2026. Its business proposition is the trading day itself rather than the matching engine.

Texas Stock Exchange first reported volume in July 2026, at 0.06 billion shares.

Four new or renamed venues in eighteen months, three of them Texan. The economics of an additional US equity exchange are well understood: Rule 611 protects a new venue's quote from the day it opens, so the venue is immediately reachable, and the marginal cost of another matching engine on proven technology is small. What is scarce is a reason for a router to send anything.

21.2 Extended and Overnight Trading

The trading day is being extended at both ends, and the constraint is not the matching engine.

NYSE Arca has filed for a 23-hour, five-day session, and the filing sets out the clock. Release 34-105532, published at 91 FR 31509 on 27 May 2026, amends Temporary Rule 7.34-E(T) to add an Overnight Trading Session that begins at 21:00 Eastern Sunday through Thursday and runs to the start of the next day's Early Trading Session at 04:00. The Late Trading Session still ends at 20:00, leaving a one-hour maintenance window from 20:00 to 21:00. Each session therefore runs 21:00 to 20:00 the following evening, which is 23 hours. The proposed implementation date is 6 December 2026. Blue Ocean's BOATS ATS has traded US equities during Asian hours for several years.

The genuinely hard problem is the SIP. A 24-hour market needs a 24-hour official NBBO, 24-hour LULD bands, and a 24-hour reference price for every rule that depends on one. The CTA and UTP Operating Committees list "the potential transition toward 24-hour SIP operations" among the concurrent obligations they are managing, alongside fractional share data, the round lot change, odd-lot depth, and the possible half-cent tick. Extending SIP hours is a plan amendment, and plan amendments normally take years.

This one did not. The Commission approved the Equity Data Plans' amendments extending the exclusive SIPs' operating hours to 23 hours a day, five days a week, and the plans told the Commission they expect to be ready to collect, consolidate, process and disseminate SIP data across the 24X Market Session by 6 December 2026, the same date NYSE Arca proposes to start. The SEC recites both facts in Release 34-106061, published at 91 FR 52756 on 14 August 2026, granting 24X National Exchange temporary conditional exemptive relief from Rule 602 through 2 July 2027. What is left is implementation, not authorisation.

21.3 The Fractional Share Problem

Retail brokers have offered fractional share trading for years and the market structure has no concept for it. A trade of 0.4 shares is not a round lot, is not an odd lot in the regulatory sense, and has no place in a Rule 605 report designed around 100-share increments.

Three changes address it. The amended Rule 605 explicitly captures execution quality information for fractional share orders. The SIPs are incorporating fractional share trading data. And the amended round lot definition, by cutting the round lot to as little as one share in stocks above 10,000 dollars, narrows the gap between the smallest quotable unit and the smallest tradable unit.

21.4 Odd Lots Become Visible

The largest genuinely completed change of the last two years is the smallest sounding. From 1 May 2026 the SIPs disseminate odd-lot information, and the amended definitions add a best odd-lot order to buy and a best odd-lot order to sell.

Before this, a 50-share order priced better than the NBBO in a 900-dollar stock was invisible on the official feed and unprotected by Rule 611. Direct feed subscribers saw it. SIP subscribers did not. The information asymmetry between the two data products was largest precisely in the most expensive and most heavily traded stocks.

The SIPs asked the SEC for a two-year exemption, to May 2028, from disseminating odd-lot quotations beyond each participant's best odd-lot bid and offer, proposing to publish the best odd-lot bid and offer across all participants plus each participant's own from May 2026. They instructed the industry to keep building for full depth of book pending a decision.

21.5 What Is Coming and What Is Stalled

In effect as of August 2026: price-tiered round lots since November 2025; odd-lot information on the SIPs since 1 May 2026; the requirement that exchange fees and rebates be determinable at execution time since the first business day of February 2026; the amended Rule 605 execution quality reports from 1 August 2026.

Adopted but not in effect: half-penny tick sizes for stocks with a time weighted average quoted spread of 0.015 dollars or less, and the reduction of the access fee cap from 30 mils to 10 mils. Both were stayed in December 2024, survived judicial review on 14 October 2025, were pushed to November 2026 by exemptive order on 31 October 2025, and were pushed again to November 2027 on 11 June 2026. Also adopted and not yet running: the official price outside 09:30 to 16:00. The SIPs are approved for 23-hour, five-day operation and are due to disseminate consolidated data across the overnight session from 6 December 2026.

Structurally unresolved: the latency gap between the SIP and direct feeds; the pricing of proprietary market data; and whether internalisation should face a competitive obligation.

The pattern across all three is the same. The technical problems in US market structure were solved a decade ago. What remains are allocation questions, and allocation questions get litigated.

23. Key Takeaways

1. An exchange is a sorted map from price to a FIFO queue, and everything else is rules about who is served first. The data structure has not changed in four hundred years. The competition is entirely about priority, and priority is a filed, SEC-approved decision rather than a technical fact.

2. Not every exchange is price-time priority, and the largest listing venue is not. Nasdaq ranks strictly by price then arrival time. The NYSE gives an order that sets a new NBBO the right to trade its full displayed quantity first, then rotates round lots among Floor Brokers, the DMM, and the electronic book on an allocation wheel. Two exchanges, two different liquidity providers being subsidised.

3. The order book does not show the demand. The worked example in Section 8 is a 50,000-share parent order in which no venue ever saw an instruction larger than 2,000 shares and no feed ever displayed more than 200. Displayed depth is an output of execution strategy, not a measure of interest.

4. Rule 611 protects the top of each venue's book and nothing else. Not depth, not non-displayed size, not reserve quantity behind an iceberg, and until November 2025 not odd lots. Nine exceptions exist, and three of them, the auction exception, the two ISO exceptions, and the one-second flickering-quote exception, define how the market actually operates.

5. Half of US share volume prints off-exchange, and most of that is retail internalisation rather than dark pools. Off-exchange volume ran between 46.54% and 52.27% every month from January 2025 through July 2026; the FINRA/Nasdaq TRF at Carteret alone carried 47.07% in July 2026. Thirty-four NMS stock ATSs account for a much smaller share than that single reporting facility.

6. The official price is not the fastest price, and that is the structural problem in US market data. The CTA SIP's processing cost averaged 19.9 microseconds on the quote feed in July 2026, which is not the issue. The two geographic hops and the top-of-book-only aggregation are. On 6 May 2010, when the mechanism was stressed, NYSE quotes were delayed over 10 seconds to CQS and just over 8 milliseconds through a proprietary feed for the same symbols in the same window.

7. The tick is the unit of competition, and it is still one cent. The SEC adopted a half-penny tier on 18 September 2024 for the 2,420 stocks with a spread of 0.015 dollars or less, about 74% of share volume. Two exchange groups petitioned, the SEC stayed the rule, the D.C. Circuit upheld it on 14 October 2025, and the compliance date has since moved twice, to November 2027. The rule exists and the market has not changed.

8. Payment for order flow is a payment for the absence of information. Retail market orders do not predict the next price move, so a wholesaler can quote inside the public spread and still profit, and pays the broker for the stream. Robinhood earned 776 million dollars of transaction-based revenue in the three months to 30 June 2026, 59% of net revenues, of which equities were 129 million and options 342 million. The equities argument is loud; the money is in options.

9. The auction is the one part of the market where speed is worthless. A call auction is a batch. It maximises executed volume, then minimises imbalance, then minimises distance from the midpoint, and every participant clears at one price. That is why the imbalance feed exists, why index funds trade at 16:00, and why the closing cross is the most heavily surveilled minute of the day.

10. The flash crash was a control-systems failure, not a market failure. An algorithm targeting 9% of trailing volume with no price limit read intermediaries passing inventory back and forth as demand, and accelerated into it. Between 14:45:13 and 14:45:27, HFTs traded over 27,000 contracts while buying 200 net. Volume is not liquidity.

11. Five seconds was enough. The CME's Stop Logic pause at 14:45:28 halted the E-Mini for five seconds and the market stabilised on resumption. Every single-stock volatility control in US equities, from the 2010 circuit breaker pilot to the permanent LULD plan, descends from that observation: the market did not need liquidity injected, it needed time for automated systems to notice that the book was empty.

12. The technical problems were solved a decade ago; what remains is allocation. Matching engines are fast, deterministic, and reliable, and the SIPs have run at 100.00% availability in nearly every quarter since 2024. The open questions are who pays for market data, whether internalisation should face a competitive obligation, what the official price is at 03:00, and how small a tick should be. None of them is an engineering question, and all of them get litigated.
